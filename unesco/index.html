Case Study · 001

UNESCO

01Overview

The creation of an identity system where UNESCO feels just as human as what it protects.

For 80 years, UNESCO has safeguarded what makes us human. Somewhere along the way, it lost touch with the people it serves. Most don’t know what UNESCO does, and those who do think it only protects monuments.

That gap became my window of opportunity: an identity that speaks to people of every background, culture, and belief, and that feels open and alive instead of rigid and cold. The following is my speculative proposal for a UNESCO rebrand.

02Foundation

An identity that excludes no one, and invites everyone.

UNESCO’s current logo is a Greco-Roman temple. It speaks from a Western point of view, a blind spot common in design, and leaves much of the world outside its frame. It also reinforces the stereotype UNESCO already struggles with: that it only protects monuments. I set out to create a symbol for all of humanity, one that holds Eastern and Western perspectives alike, excludes no one, and invites everyone.

I started by looking outward. What connects us as humans? What patterns do we share? Patterns. Shapes. Repetition. Weaving. Seen across humanity, textiles carry our stories, our pasts, and our futures. The interconnectedness of weaving became the emotional anchor of this rebrand.

03Interaction

A digital window into the world happening all around you.

To mark UNESCO’s 80th anniversary, I designed and built the Digital Atlas, an installation meant to be touched as much as seen. A tabletop map presents the four worlds UNESCO protects: languages, ecosystems, traditions, and monuments. When visitors place an icon on the map, the overhead display opens a window into that piece of humanity. The Digital Atlas shows how connected we are, how distinct we are, and why what makes us human is worth preserving.

04Palette

A hue palette that is an homage to our shared home. Planet Earth.

The palette has two sides: what can be seen and what can be felt. World Blue, Vivre Green, and Sol Yellow stand for knowledge, life, and culture. Ash Grey, Umber Brown, and Deep Earth stand for memory, ancestry, and heritage. Together they hold the vibrancy of human expression and the depth of human experience: the hues of our shared home, woven into the fabric of humanity.

05Iconography

Taken apart and rebuilt, the new logo became so much more than one single asset.

True to the craft of textiles, I unraveled the logo and wove it back together, using only its own elements. Taken apart and rebuilt, it became icons for the four worlds UNESCO protects: languages, ecosystems, traditions, and monuments. Then I pushed it further into a cast of ten characters who live throughout the system. I call them my new little friends.

06Typography

A system must be able to flex. So, how far can the logo be pushed?

Well, I kept asking “what if?” of the modular logo, holding to one rule: no new shapes, only what the logo is made of. That rule produced two typefaces for the identity system: Building Blocks and Woven Textiles.

07Applications

The system then is carried into both spatial, ephemeral and digital media.

The World Needs U.

The slogan plays on the four U’s in the logo, but it reaches past UNESCO to the world it serves: us. Organizations like UNESCO exist for the generations that come after us. It’s on all of us to carry our stories, cultures, and traditions into the future, not leave them behind. The world needs U.

Safeguard Humanity’s Inheritance.
Our inheritance is all around us, in the identities we hold today. Without intention, it can be lost.

Protect/Preserve/Prepare What Makes Us Human.
Protect who we are today. Preserve what the past has given us. Prepare what we pass on. This is why the rebrand matters: we cannot forget who we are, or what shaped us.

I think, create, and design this way because of my Peruvian and Salvadoran heritage. This is my perspective, and that is the beauty of who we are, as individuals and as a collective. Every perspective is needed to see the whole picture. Without our cultures, our stories, and our identities, humanity would be a shell of itself.

As part of the identity system, I also designed a new digital home for UNESCO, one that puts the people, lands, and cultures it protects at the center. I left the corporate look behind for something inviting, youthful, and vibrant. My goal was for every page to offer something new, so visitors would keep wanting to discover more.

08Tangible Forms

80 years of human connection. What an accomplishment.

To honor 80 years of UNESCO, I wanted objects you could hold and contemplate, built around what I see as UNESCO’s greatest accomplishment: human connection. I commissioned a woven blanket and, wanting in on the fun, I personally hand-sewed a vegetable-tanned leather bag, its icons coming together to form an image of our world. The same image lives in the printed atlas.

You never know where one small idea will take you. That’s the beauty of design. It is vital to my process to never throw an idea away. I keep it close, in my sketches. Perhaps it returns in a way I hadn’t imagined.

09Campaign Direction

The creation of a campaign hosted by UNESCO, “Living Traces”.

In direct contrast to the identity system, I created a campaign about the languages, traditions, and cultures at risk of disappearing. The subject is urgent, so I stripped away all color and made the campaign impossible to ignore.

The name Living Traces holds two truths: these cultures are still alive, but only as traces. Once they fade, they may never return. The campaign brings light to what is nearly lost and asks the world to do its part: to protect our cultures, and our neighbors’, as we move into the future.

10The Printed Atlas

Before the Digital Atlas, there was the printed one.

It begins as a celebration of our differences: the languages we speak, the lands we tend, the rituals we keep, the monuments we raise. But turn the pages, and something quieter surfaces. Somewhere between the words and the traditions, you start to see yourself in a stranger. Because before anything else, you and I are human.

Open the atlas and the world unfolds in your hands. Four icons mark the four worlds UNESCO protects. Each world then appears on its own, with a map where every icon carries a number: the page where that place can be found. Each map becomes a table of contents for the globe.
